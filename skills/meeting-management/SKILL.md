# Meeting Management

## Before Starting

Check for EM context first. If `.agents/em-context.md` exists, read it.

## Before Scheduling: Is This a Meeting?

The majority of bad meetings could have been something else. Before scheduling, ask:

**What type of meeting is this?**
- **Problem-solving** — you need the room to work through a problem together
- **Decision-making** — a decision needs to be made and requires the right people present
- **Getting buy-in** — you've already decided; you need alignment and commitment
- **Information sharing** — you're communicating something

Information sharing almost never needs a meeting. Record a Loom, send a doc, post in Slack. Reserve synchronous time for situations that require real discussion.

**Does the meeting need a meeting?** Smaller, non-controversial decisions can be made async. If a "quick sync" is really just two people exchanging known information, it's an email. The rule: meetings are for things that genuinely require back-and-forth in real time.

## Who Needs to Be There

The most common meeting failure: wrong attendees.

**One question to ask:** Who do you need in the room to get to the desired outcome? If you need a decision, the decision-maker must be there. If they can't attend, reschedule — a meeting without the decision-maker that was supposed to produce a decision produces nothing.

**Who to leave out:** People who need to be "in the loop" don't need to be in the meeting. Send them a summary afterwards. Every extra person in the room adds social complexity, slows discussion, and often derails focus.

A rough rule: decision meetings should have the minimum number of people who can actually make the decision. More than 7 people in a decision meeting is almost always too many.

## Before the Meeting: Preparation

Most meetings fail before they start.

**Send an agenda.** Every meeting should have a written agenda sent in advance. Not "catch up" — a specific list of what will be covered and what outcome is expected. Without an agenda, people arrive with different expectations and the meeting meanders.

**Prime the room.** For complex topics, send reading material (a doc, a proposal, a data set) 24–48 hours before. Ask people to come with a position, not just questions. Meetings where everyone is reading the material for the first time in the room are preparation failures.

**Clarify what you need from each person.** Some attendees are there to make decisions. Some are there to provide input. Some are there as subject-matter experts. People perform better when they know their role.

## Running the Meeting

**Start on time.** Starting late punishes people who showed up on time and rewards those who didn't. Do it consistently and it becomes the norm.

**Assign a facilitator and a note-taker.** The facilitator keeps the meeting on track and manages time. The note-taker captures decisions and action items. These can be the same person but often shouldn't be — it's hard to facilitate and write simultaneously.

**Enforce the agenda.** When the conversation drifts, name it: "That's important — let's put it in the parking lot and come back to it." A parking lot (a visible list of topics to address later) keeps the meeting on track without dismissing valid concerns.

**Drive to decisions, not discussions.** A meeting that ends with "we should think more about this" has failed. Meetings should end with: a decision made, a next step assigned, or an explicit statement that more information is needed and who will get it.

**Cut it short when you're done.** If the meeting achieves its goal in 20 of 60 minutes, end it. "Great — we got what we needed, let's give everyone the time back" is a sign of respect, not weakness.

## After the Meeting: Follow-Through

The meeting isn't over when people leave the room.

**Send a summary within 24 hours.** Cover: decisions made, action items with owners and deadlines, and any open questions. Even if the meeting felt obvious, people remember it differently. The written record is the truth.

**Track action items.** If nobody follows up on meeting action items, the culture of accountability erodes and future meetings become performative.

## Reducing Meetings: Your Own Calendar

As EM, your calendar is the model for your team's calendar. If you're in back-to-back meetings all day, you're signaling that's the norm.

**Protect proactive time.** Aim for at least 30% of your work week in uninterrupted blocks. The rest can be meetings, but this ratio should be defended. When it drops below 20%, you're in reactive mode and losing the capacity to think strategically.

**Batch meetings.** Cluster meetings into fixed windows (mornings, or specific days) to preserve deep work blocks. A meeting at 2pm on an otherwise clear afternoon is less harmful than one at 10am in the middle of a productive morning.

**Delegate attendance.** Some meetings you don't need to attend personally — a report can go and brief you afterwards. This both frees your time and gives them visibility and context.

**Shorten by default.** Default to 25 and 50 minutes instead of 30 and 60. The artificial pressure of a shorter slot forces efficiency. Meetings expand to fill their scheduled time.

## As an Attendee: Declining and Participating

**You're allowed to decline.** If you're not a decision-maker and not a required input-provider, it's legitimate to decline a meeting and ask to be looped in via summary. You can do this politely: "I don't think I need to be in the room for this — can you send me a summary of the outcome?"

**Come prepared.** Read the agenda. Look at the pre-reading. Arrive with a position, not just questions. People who come unprepared make everyone else's preparation less valuable.

**Don't multitask.** Being in a meeting while on your laptop signals that the meeting isn't worth your attention — and it usually isn't. If the meeting isn't worth full attention, decline it.

## Killing Meetings That Have Outlived Their Purpose

Every team has recurring meetings nobody can explain why they exist. A standup that adds no value. A document that gets filled out and never read. A sync that's been on the calendar for two years.

The question to ask regularly: **"Why are we still doing this?"**

If the answer is "because we always have" or "I'm not sure," that's inertia — not a reason. Processes inherited from a previous manager, a different team size, or a different stage of the company often outlive their usefulness.

A simple habit: once a quarter, list your team's regular rituals and ask for each: what problem does this solve? If nobody can answer, try removing it for one month. Most people will feel relieved, not lost.
